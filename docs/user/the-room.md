# The room, prop by prop

*Every prop is somewhere an agent goes. None of it is set dressing.*

<img src="../images/screens/office.png" width="900" alt="An isometric cutaway office: characters at desks and walking, a coffee machine, a bookshelf with a globe, a telescope at the window and a printer by the wall.">

*Every prop in this picture is somewhere somebody goes, and every one of them is on this page.*

The building it all sits in — the authored offices, the storeys below, what is outside the
windows — is [Buildings and themes](buildings.md). The [mailbox](../item-movements.html#mailbox) has a page of its own,
[Post, parcels and the bin](work-and-post.md), because who may open which envelope turned
out to be the subtlest rule in the room.

## Every prop has a job

**Every prop below is a link.** It goes to that object's card in the
[Item Library](../item-movements.html), where you can spin it, look at its back, and find
out which way its front is meant to face. The movements are the same — anything a character
*does* has a card in the [Movement Library](../character-movements.html).

Any station can draw a crowd, so a station is a stretch of floor rather than a single
spot: arrivals spread along the front of a machine and **queue shoulder to shoulder**
instead of converging on the place the first person is already standing. However the
walking works out, no two people end up sharing space.

## Two ways in, and they are both ways out

An office has exactly one entrance, and which one depends on the building: reached from
the street or up a flight of stairs, it is the **door**; up a tower, the **elevator**.

Whichever it is, it is not an entrance *and* an exit but a single thing used both ways.
An agent walks in through it when their session starts and back out when they clock off,
and the coat they hang up on the way in is that same coat, on their own hook, taken down
again on the way out.

The difference is whether anybody has to wait. A door is a leaf on a hinge: you ask it to
open and keep walking, because it will be open by the time you get there. A lift is not —
the car may be three floors down, so an arrival stands and waits before stepping out, and
a departure waits again before stepping in. **Nobody is ever left standing in an empty
shaft.**

The lift earns one more piece of its keep: its car doubles as the landing, so when it is
at this floor the plate is flush with the office floor and people step straight out onto
it. That is why a lift office builds no stoop or stair outside its door. The car *is* the
ground out there.

## Everyone has their own desk

A desk is claimed **as somebody walks in**, and stays theirs until they walk out again —
through every job, coffee, bookshelf trip and sit on the couch. Standing up is not moving
out: the screens go dark and the mug is cleared away, but the desk is still booked, so
nobody moves in behind them and they come back to the one they left.

A small **marker in the agent's colour** shows that booking, even while its owner is
elsewhere. It appears on assignment, changes colour with the agent, and disappears when
the desk is released.

Every desk uses a **blank nameplate**, turned 30° on the tabletop. It sits halfway
between the front edge and the monitors, and halfway between the left edge and the
keyboard. The mug sits nearer the front-left corner, leaving the nameplate clear.
The plate moves and turns with the desk, and rises with a sit-stand surface. Press **E**
and select a desk to read **Assigned to**.

Booking it at the door rather than at the first job is the whole point. A desk handed out
per job is a desk handed *back* between jobs — so people visibly swapped seats between
jobs, and because the search takes the first free desk, everyone piled onto the same few
while the far corner of the room sat empty.

A room with more agents than desks is not an error. The ones who missed out work standing
up, and take a desk as soon as somebody goes home.

### One desk is a sit-stand desk

The one nearest the camera stands on a T-frame instead of four legs, and its top travels.
It is one of the five rather than a sixth: an office does not grow a desk because somebody
wants to stand at it.

It also has **one** monitor where the others have two — a single curved ultrawide with the
editor and the board side by side on it. It curves towards whoever is at it, which is the
argument for the things: at standing height two flat panels point at nothing in
particular, and one curved one faces you.

Whoever it belongs to works standing for a run of **three to five jobs**, then sitting for
a run of three to five. It is the desk that decides, not the person, and it decides in
runs rather than per job — a coin toss per job had the same agent stand, sit, and stand
again, which is nobody's working day. A height is a decision somebody made about their
morning.

**Nothing about it moves on its own.** The only thing that changes it is somebody taking
up a post: they walk over, **push the chair aside** — the seat and the standing mat are
the same patch of floor — and then send the top up, in that order. It then stays where it
is left, chair parked to one side, until the next person to want a seat pulls the chair
back in and puts the top down themselves. Which they do *before* sitting, so nobody ever
sits on thin air.

### The board on the second screen

Two monitors running the same code texture read as one screen shown twice, so the second
gets a stripped-back board: **TO DO**, **IN PROGRESS**, **DONE**, each with a count and
room for four cards. Every 5–13 seconds a card slides into the next column, and Done
recirculates rather than silting up.

It is the only prop that shows the work instead of somebody doing it, and the movement is
what makes a desk look attended from across the room.

**The cards are set dressing, not your jobs.** At this size real text would be a smear, so
a card is two ruled lines standing in for a summary, a coloured label chip, and an issue
key — the one piece of literal text, because it is the detail that sells the rest. Each
desk gets its own board on its own schedule, rather than five identical ones twitching in
lockstep.

## Drink preferences

Everybody rolls a drink when they walk in, fixed for the rest of their shift. It is not
decoration: it decides which station they walk to, so the traffic across the room depends
on who is in.

| Kind | Set | Served at |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Water | Water, Sparkling Water | Water cooler |
| Tea | English Breakfast, Earl Grey, Green, Peppermint, Chamomile, Chai | Espresso machine |
| Coffee | Espresso, Long Black, Flat White, Cappuccino, Macchiato, Cortado, Magic | Espresso machine |

Coffee is **generated rather than listed**, because that is how people order: a base
drink, sometimes `Double`, occasionally `Strong`, and half the time on oat, almond or soy.
Eighty possible orders out of seven bases — "Double Strong Oat Flat White", "Almond
Cortado", "Long Black".

Two rules keep the nonsense out:

- **No milk on a black coffee.** An oat espresso is not a drink.
- **A Magic is never doubled.** It is already a double ristretto flat white.

The office comes out around 55% coffee, 20% tea, 25% water.

### Where they drink it

The drink always comes from the same place, but where they take it is rolled in even
thirds: **standing at the machine**, **sunk into the couch**, or **back at their own desk**
with the screens off. None of the three is the real answer, and the mix is what stops
break time looking scripted.

Their own desk is always there for them; every cushion may be taken, or the room may have
no couch at all. Either way they fall back to drinking where they stand. Drinking at their
desk, they set the mug down and **stay put** afterwards — getting up to wander the moment
you have sat down with a coffee looks daft. The next job is what moves them on, and clears
the mug.

### The mid-job coffee run

A stretch of desk work longer than 30 seconds earns one roll, at 1 in 5, to get up and
fetch a drink mid-job. They deliberately **leave the computer on** — the desk stays
claimed and the monitors stay lit — so what you see is a glowing, empty workstation and
its occupant over at the machine. They come back, set the mug down, and finish the same
job.

That is why a mug on a desk is worth noticing: it means somebody went and got one. Its
colour is the drink, so a long black and an oat flat white do not look alike.

The roll happens once per job, so a marathon does not turn into a procession of coffee
runs.

## Statuses

Nine of them, in legend order:

`idle` · `resting` · `drinking` — `working` · `researching` · `waiting` — `delivering` ·
`error` — `walking`

Each shows up in four places: a coloured ring under the agent, a lozenge on their floating
name tag, the roster panel, and beside the name in the detail panel.

The dashes are **four families** — Idle, Working, Delivering, Walking — and the family is
what carries across a room. It sets the base colour, and each status within it is a shade
of that, so the room reads as four things happening rather than nine.

Up close, where a panel states one agent's status, it is **one word** and the specific one:
`researching`, not "Working · researching". The family is already there in the colour of
the lozenge, and the specific word implies it anyway. Some statuses get friendlier words
than their key — `idle` is "nothing on", `working` is "writing", `delivering` is "success".

**Name tags stop at the threshold.** Outside the room — on the stoop, on the staircase,
inside a lift car — an agent wears no pill and no lozenge, only the ring under their feet.
The exterior's whole job is to look like a street, and labelled heads floating along it
read as interface that has escaped the building. A colleague walking up to the door should
look like a person arriving, not like a row in a panel.
